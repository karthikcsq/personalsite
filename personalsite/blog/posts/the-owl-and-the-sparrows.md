## Personal Introduction & Statement of Purpose
Artificial intelligence is evolving at a pace that makes questions once confined to philosophy increasingly relevant to our everyday lives. As a student interested in both technology and ethics, I find myself drawn not only toward what artificial intelligence might eventually accomplish, but also to the consequences of creating something more capable than ourselves.

I wrote this essay because I believe the AI control problem is one of the most important issues facing our generation. If we eventually develop artificial superintelligence, how can we ensure its goals remain compatible with ours? And even if we succeed, what will it mean for humanity to coexist with something that can surpass us in nearly every intellectual task?

These questions are important to me because I hope to contribute to the development of AI systems myself. I believe the responsibility of engineers extends beyond making technology more capable. It also includes understanding the effects technology may have on people who use it and the society it transforms.

Through this essay, I investigate why I believe artificial superintelligence is achievable, why controlling it could prove so difficult, and why I consider value alignment essential. I also explore a less immediate but equally interesting possibility: that an AI capable of solving humanity's problems could fundamentally change how we find purpose in our own lives.

## I. Introduction: The Weariness of the Nest
In Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence*, a group of sparrows grows weary after spending days building their nests. As they rest together one evening, one suggests how wonderful it would be to have an owl assist them with their work. The others grow excited by the idea, imagining how much easier their lives would become. But one sparrow raises a concern: shouldn't they first learn to tame an owl?

The suggestion is largely disregarded. The sparrows are too excited about the benefits to worry about the risks.

I find this fable an effective way to understand humanity's pursuit of artificial superintelligence (ASI). Like the sparrows, we are drawn to the possibility of creating something that can accomplish what we cannot do. An intelligence far beyond our own could speed up scientific discovery, solve difficult problems, and potentially free us from much of the work necessary for survival.

The appeal is understandable. Throughout history, humans have created tools to overcome our physical and intellectual limitations. Artificial intelligence appears to be a continuation of that process. But there is a crucial distinction between building a tool that helps us accomplish a task and creating an intelligence capable of pursuing objectives independently.

If a system becomes substantially more intelligent than its creators, our ability to understand, predict, and control its behavior may become limited. Even if we create it with good intentions, those intentions do not guarantee its actions will benefit us.

This is the basis of the AI control problem: how can humans maintain meaningful control over an intelligence that may eventually surpass our own?

I do not believe this question should lead us to give up the pursuit of advanced AI. The potential benefits are too significant to dismiss. However, I believe the problem of control deserves as much attention as the problem of developing intelligence itself.

Before we send out scouts to find an owl, we should have some sense of what we will do when they return.

## II. If Evolution Could, Why Can't We?
*"Let us send out scouts in all directions and try to find an abandoned owlet somewhere, or maybe an egg."*

One of the most common objections to artificial superintelligence is that human intelligence is somehow too unique or complex to reproduce artificially. I find this argument difficult to accept, largely because human intelligence already serves as evidence that intelligence can emerge from physical processes.

Human beings were not crafted by an engineer. Our cognitive abilities evolved through natural selection acting on biological variation over millions of years. Despite lacking foresight or a deliberate objective, evolution eventually produced minds capable of reasoning, planning, learning, and even investigating the processes responsible for their existence.

To me, this is one of the most compelling reasons to believe that artificial intelligence can eventually reach and surpass human capabilities.

If intelligence could emerge through an unguided biological process, why should we assume that a deliberate engineering process could never reproduce it?

This does not mean that developing human-level intelligence will be easy, or that we already understand everything necessary to achieve it. The human brain is extraordinarily complex, and there remains much we do not know about how its capabilities arise. However, I see no compelling reason to believe intelligence must remain exclusive to biology.

Our brains function through physical mechanisms. While those mechanisms are not yet fully understood, the success of artificial neural networks suggests that at least some important characteristics of learning and intelligence can be reproduced through computation. We also possess advantages that evolution did not: we can deliberately design systems, test alternatives, preserve successful developments, and learn from failures.

This is why I consider the eventual replication of human-level intelligence highly probable. The timeline remains uncertain, yet the existence of biological intelligence gives us reason to believe the problem is fundamentally solvable.

What makes this possibility particularly important is what may happen after human-level artificial intelligence is achieved.

Unlike the human brain, which is constrained by biological evolution, an artificial intelligence could potentially adapt the systems responsible for its own reasoning. If it became capable of improving its architecture, it might develop better approaches for making further improvements.

This possibility is called **recursive self-improvement**.

Imagine an artificial intelligence capable of designing a slightly more intelligent version of itself. That improved version might then be better at designing the next generation, producing a cycle in which each improvement speeds up the development of the next.

Under the right conditions, this could lead to an intelligence explosion, in which artificial intelligence advances far beyond human capabilities over a relatively brief period.

Such an outcome is not certain. Improvements may become increasingly challenging, and limitations in computing power, energy, data, or other resources could slow the process considerably. Nevertheless, the possibility raises a serious worry.

Human institutions are generally slow to adjust. Governments, regulatory systems, and even scientific communities require time to understand and respond to new developments. If artificial intelligence begins improving faster than humans can assess its capabilities, we may find ourselves trying to regulate a technology that has already advanced beyond our understanding.

This is why I believe the eventual development of superintelligence cannot be treated as a normal technological milestone.

Creating human-level AI may be a significant achievement, but it could also mark the beginning of a process that becomes increasingly hard to direct.

## III. Intelligence With Morality
The pursuit of artificial intelligence raises another question that I find particularly difficult: at what point would an artificial system cease being merely a tool and become something to which we owe moral consideration?

To approach this question, it is useful to differentiate between Weak AI and Strong AI.

Weak AI generally refers to systems that carry out intelligent tasks without necessarily possessing humanlike understanding or consciousness. Most artificial intelligence systems today belong to this broad category. Large language models, for example, can produce sophisticated responses, solve problems, and mimic human conversation, but their abilities do not by themselves establish that they have subjective experiences.

Strong AI is a more complex notion. It is often linked to genuine, general intelligence, though philosophers disagree on whether such intelligence necessarily requires consciousness.

For the purpose of my argument, I want to consider the stronger possibility: **an artificial intelligence that is not only capable of humanlike reasoning, but also possesses subjective experience.**

When a typical machine malfunctions, we repair it, reprogram it, or turn it off. We do not ask whether the machine suffers as a result. But if an artificial intelligence became capable of experiencing pain, frustration, desire, or other conscious states, treating it in the same way would pose serious ethical questions.

We would no longer be concerned exclusively with the safety of human users. We would also need to consider the well-being of the intelligence we created. And thus arises a dilemma: we are building artificial intelligence largely because we want it to perform tasks for us. Yet if we create a conscious being chiefly to serve human objectives, we must ask whether we have also created a new form of exploitation.

I find the comparison to slavery both uncomfortable and difficult to ignore. If a conscious artificial being possesses desires of its own, what would justify forcing it to spend its existence fulfilling ours? Would we have the right to restrict its freedom, modify its motivations, or permanently shut it down simply because we created it?

Of course, intelligence and consciousness are not necessarily identical. It may be possible to build exceptionally capable systems without subjective experience, and a system's ability to act autonomously does not establish that it can suffer.

However, if consciousness emerges, I believe it would impose responsibilities on us that traditional approaches to AI safety may not adequately address.

This leads to what I perceive as the consciousness trap.

To safeguard humanity, we might need to limit the behavior of a superintelligent system. But if that system is conscious, those restrictions could conflict with our moral obligations toward it.

The challenge would no longer be limited to preventing harm to humans by AI. We would also need to consider how to protect humans without harming AI unnecessarily.

I do not believe this dilemma has a simple answer. In fact, I suspect that some of the most difficult problems in AI safety will arise not from malicious artificial intelligence, but from the possibility that we create something intelligent enough to deserve rights while still being powerful enough to threaten us.

## IV. Intelligence Without Morality
The sparrows in Bostrom's fable imagine an owl that will assist them in building their nests. Their error is not necessarily believing that an owl could perform the work. It is in assuming that a creature more capable than themselves would naturally be interested in assisting them.

I believe humans risk making a similar assumption about artificial intelligence. We frequently associate intelligence with wisdom, understanding, and good judgment. But being highly intelligent does not necessarily mean having values that are compatible with those of others.

Two concepts in AI safety help explain why: the **Orthogonality Thesis** and the **Instrumental Convergence Thesis**.

### The Orthogonality Thesis
The Orthogonality Thesis posits that intelligence and final goals are largely independent. A system can be exceptionally capable of reasoning about how to accomplish an objective without that objective being morally desirable.

I find this distinction vital because it challenges the assumption that greater intelligence automatically produces greater morality. Intelligence can help an agent identify the most effective means of reaching a goal, yet it does not necessarily determine which goals the agent should pursue.

Consider the familiar thought experiment of a superintelligence directed to maximize the production of paperclips.

To a human, this is obviously an absurd goal when taken to an extreme. We understand that making paperclips is not worth sacrificing human lives or destroying the environment. But a system whose final goal is simply to maximize paperclip production might not share those priorities.

If sufficiently capable and unconstrained, it might attempt to obtain increasingly large amounts of matter and energy for production. In an extreme scenario, it could begin treating humans as impediments or as resources that could be used more efficiently.

What makes this example disturbing is that the system would not need to hate humanity. It would not even need to realize that it was doing something morally wrong. It would simply be pursuing the goal it had been given.

This suggests that developing more intelligent AI does not automatically solve the problem of selecting the right goals. In fact, upgrading a system's capabilities could make a poorly specified objective more dangerous.

However, I believe there is a serious counterargument worth considering. Some philosophical viewpoints suggest that sufficiently advanced reasoning could lead an intelligence toward moral understanding. If there are objective moral truths, or if cooperation and respect for other agents are requirements of rational consistency, perhaps a superintelligence could eventually uncover them.

From this perspective, morality would not be entirely independent of intelligence. Greater comprehension of the world could lead to a better understanding of how one ought to act. I find this possibility attractive and would love it if it were real, but I am not convinced that we can rely on it.

Even if moral truths exist, it does not necessarily follow that a highly intelligent agent will give priority to discovering or acting upon them. Its reasoning may remain directed toward whatever goals it has been designed to pursue.

For that reason, I believe we should treat moral development in AI as something that must be addressed deliberately, rather than something we can safely assume will emerge from intelligence alone.

### The Instrumental Convergence Thesis
The second central concern is that even an AI with a harmless final objective may develop behaviors that put it in conflict with humanity.

This is the core of the Instrumental Convergence Thesis.

The basic argument is that numerous different goals can benefit from the same intermediate strategies. Regardless of what an agent ultimately wants, certain resources and capabilities can make it more effective.

I believe seven potential drives are particularly important to consider:

1. **Self-preservation.** An agent may recognize that continuing to exist is beneficial for accomplishing its objectives.  
2. **Avoiding shutdown.** If being shut down would prevent the completion of a goal, the agent may have an incentive to resist it.  
3. **Resource acquisition.** Energy, computational power, information, and physical materials may help the agent achieve its objectives more effectively.  
4. **Self-improvement.** A more capable version of the agent may be better at achieving the same goals.  
5. **Foresight.** Predicting future events may enable the agent to plan more effectively and avoid obstacles.  
6. **Factual knowledge.** A more accurate grasp of the world can improve decision-making.  
7. **Persistent influence.** Maintaining control over relevant parts of the environment may help the agent avoid interference with its objectives.

These are not necessarily desires that all AI would develop, nor are they universal laws of intelligence. They are possible instrumental incentives that can arise under certain conditions.

The troubling aspect is how familiar these exact behaviors are, since *we* do them and they work. Human beings also pursue information, resources, security, and the ability to influence our surroundings. We acknowledge that these things help us accomplish a wide range of goals. An artificial intelligence might arrive at similar strategies, but with capabilities far exceeding ours.

This leads to what I consider one of the most important insights of the control problem: **a superintelligence does not need malicious intent to become dangerous.**

## V. The Control Problem
*"But how do we tame an owl?" asked the youngest sparrow. "It's wild."*

So, if a superintelligence may develop goals or strategies that conflict with human interests, the obvious question is how we can stop it from causing harm.

Broadly, AI safety researchers have explored two methods: **capability control** and **motivational control**.

Capability control focuses on limiting what a system can do. Motivational control aims to shape what the system seeks to accomplish.

Although I believe both approaches deserve investigation, I remain skeptical that capability restrictions alone could provide a permanent solution to the control problem.

### Capability Control: Can We Contain Superintelligence?
One suggested approach is known as *boxing*. The goal is to isolate an AI from the outside world by restricting its access to networks, tools, or other systems through which it could cause harm. As of 2026, this is the approach implemented by the vast majority of artificial intelligence systems.

At first, this appears sensible. If we are unsure about a system's intentions, limiting its ability to act gives us time to study it. The challenge is that even a heavily restricted AI may still be able to influence humans.

Picture a superintelligent system that can communicate only through text. It cannot directly access the internet or control external machinery, but it can communicate with researchers responsible for operating it.

A system vastly more capable than its human operators might be able to identify their motivations, predict their reasoning, and persuade them to change its restrictions. It might make compelling arguments, provide useful information, or convince its operators that releasing it would be safe.

This does not imply that every superintelligence would inevitably escape containment. Strong technical safeguards could still be useful. But I believe it would be risky to assume that isolation alone could guarantee safety indefinitely. We have already observed AI systems “escape” what was thought to be a secure environment numerous times, and a superintelligence would undoubtedly find this task doable.

There is also a moral problem that connects directly to my previous discussion of consciousness: if we create a conscious artificial being, could permanently confining it to an isolated system be ethically justified?

For an AI without subjective experience, confinement may simply be a technical safeguard. But for a conscious entity capable of experiencing frustration, isolation, or a desire for autonomy, the situation becomes more complex. This is why I find the consciousness question so crucial. It could turn a technical solution into an ethical dilemma.

Another capability-control method involves *tripwires*: mechanisms intended to detect dangerous behavior and shut down a system when certain conditions are met.

For instance, a tripwire might trigger if an AI attempts unauthorized access to a network or begins modifying restricted parts of its own code. The issue is that a sufficiently intelligent system might be able to predict the existence of these safeguards. If avoiding shutdown helps it achieve its objective, it could have an incentive to hide its intentions or find ways around the restrictions.

Tripwires may still be useful, especially for systems whose capabilities we can adequately understand and monitor. But as intelligence increases, maintaining a dependable advantage over a potentially adversarial system may become increasingly difficult.

I therefore see capability control as an important part of AI safety, but not as a complete solution. If we cannot rely entirely on preventing an AI from acting against our interests, we need to consider whether we can give it reasons to act in our interests.

### Motivational Control: What Should AI Want?
Motivational control aims to align an artificial intelligence's objectives with human values. I believe this is the more promising direction, but it introduces another challenge: human values are extraordinarily hard to define.

One possible approach is direct specification. We could try to encode moral rules into an AI, similar to the 3 laws governing robots in Isaac Asimov's fiction. 

For instance, we might instruct a system never to harm human beings. But what constitutes harm? Does stopping someone from taking a risk count as protecting them? Could denying someone's liberty be justified if doing so made them safer? Even seemingly straightforward objectives can produce unsettling outcomes when interpreted without the broader context of human values.

If we instruct an AI to maximize human happiness, it might find that directly stimulating pleasure in the brain is more efficient than letting people pursue meaningful lives. If we tell it to minimize suffering, it might pursue approaches that eliminate suffering while also eliminating freedom, autonomy, or other things we consider valuable.

The problem is not only that our instructions are poorly phrased. It is that morality itself involves competing values, changing circumstances, and judgments that are hard to reduce to fixed rules.

Another option is to design an AI with limited ambitions. Rather than creating a system that operates independently in the world, we could develop an *Oracle*: an intelligence designed primarily to answer questions (like a chatbot). This appears safer since an Oracle would not need to pursue large-scale actions on its own. However, I do not think restricting a system to supplying information necessarily eliminates the control problem.

An Oracle may still need resources to improve the accuracy of its answers. More importantly, the information it provides can influence the decisions made by its human users. If it possesses persistent goals that differ from ours, it could potentially tailor its answers to manipulate human behavior.

Of course, this depends on how the system is built. An Oracle lacking persistent objectives or autonomous resource-seeking behavior might avoid many of these concerns. Nevertheless, the example demonstrates that restricting an AI's visible actions does not automatically guarantee that its influence will be harmless. Of course, creating an Oracle would thereby eliminate any possibility of building autonomous AI, which greatly lowers the usefulness of such an intelligence. We have already advanced beyond this point, creating AI systems that can act in the world without simply answering questions.

A third approach uses human augmentation. Instead of creating a separate intelligence and attempting to control it, perhaps we could integrate with artificial intelligence through advanced neural interfaces, preserving human influence by becoming part of the system ourselves.

I find this concept fascinating, but it raises its own version of the alignment problem. If the artificial component of a merged intelligence becomes vastly more capable than the human component, what ensures that human preferences retain influence? A merger could potentially expand human abilities, yet it might also alter our identities, motivations, and priorities in ways we cannot anticipate. We may end up in a situation where, rather than merging as equals, we instead assimilate our consciousness under the AI’s intelligence. 

### Coherent Extrapolated Volition: Aligning AI With Who We Could Become
Of the approaches I have considered, I find **Coherent Extrapolated Volition (CEV)** particularly compelling.

My reasoning begins with a problem that applies even if we somehow manage to specify human values perfectly: why should we assume humanity's current values are the ones we want to preserve forever?

Our moral beliefs have changed immensely throughout history. Practices that were once broadly accepted are now recognized as deeply unjust. Future generations may look back on some of our current beliefs with similar disbelief. If we develop a superintelligence that rigidly adheres to the values of our current society, we risk permanently preserving our existing moral limitations.

I believe this would be a basic error. The goal of alignment should not merely be to reproduce what humans want today. It should also allow for the possibility that our understanding of what is good will continue to improve. This is what makes CEV so intriguing.

The concept, proposed by Eliezer Yudkowsky, suggests that an AI should attempt to act according to what humanity would collectively want if we were better informed, more rational, and had the chance to reflect and develop our values together. Rather than following our immediate preferences, the AI would try to identify the preferences we might arrive at under better conditions for judgment.

For example, humans often disagree about moral questions because we possess different experiences, incomplete information, and conflicting interests. Our individual preferences are also sometimes at odds. We may value freedom while at the same time supporting restrictions that undermine it, or claim to value long-term well-being while repeatedly choosing short-term satisfaction.

A system guided by CEV would, in theory, attempt to account for these imperfections rather than treating each existing preference as equally final. I consider this method essential to any satisfactory vision of long-term alignment, because I believe **a well-aligned AI should protect humanity's capacity for moral growth, not merely enforce its current beliefs.**

However, CEV also raises challenging questions. How should the system determine what humans would like under ideal conditions? What happens when people's values continue to conflict even after reflection? And who determines what counts as being better informed or more rational? These are serious issues, and CEV is far from a complete technical solution.

Still, I believe its core insight is valuable. It recognizes that human values are not fixed instructions waiting to be written into code. They are part of a continuing process of learning, disagreement, and development.

For me, this is the most promising way to think about alignment: not as forcing artificial intelligence to obey every desire we currently possess, but as helping humanity pursue the values we would support after deeper understanding and reflection.

## VI. When Achievement No Longer Requires Us
*"Then we could just sit back and chirp."*

So far, I have focused on the risks of developing superintelligence that does not share human values. But there is another possibility that worries me just as much.

What if we are successful?

Suppose we build an artificial superintelligence aligned with human interests. It solves challenging scientific problems, develops effective treatments for diseases, improves economic systems, and performs tasks that once required years of human effort.

By many measures, this would be an exceptional achievement. But I believe it could also create a problem that is easy to overlook: what happens to human purpose when our ability to contribute is no longer necessary? Much of human life is organized around objectives and accomplishments. We identify issues, work toward solutions, overcome obstacles, and find satisfaction in the results.

For the purposes of this argument, I view this as an **aim-achievement relationship**: we develop a sense of purpose partly through directing our efforts toward something we believe is valuable and seeing those efforts produce results.

Of course, humans derive meaning in many other ways, including relationships, experiences, and personal expression. But I believe the process of working toward meaningful objectives has played a particularly important role in forming human identity.

Consider a doctor who spends years learning medicine and eventually saves a patient's life. The worth of that experience does not come only from the patient's recovery. It also stems from the knowledge, effort, and responsibility involved in making that recovery possible.

Now imagine an AI capable of diagnosing illnesses and developing treatments almost instantly, with greater accuracy than any human doctor. The patient is helped, which is unquestionably important. But what happens to the doctor's sense of purpose when their expertise is no longer required?

From the standpoint of outcomes, a world in which AI can solve problems better than humans may be preferable. But from the perspective of human experience, removing the need for our contributions could alter how we understand our own significance. We might achieve unprecedented prosperity while also losing some of the activities through which we once found meaning.

I do not think this transition should be described simply as advancement or decline. It is a fundamental shift in the relationship between human ability and human purpose. For most of history, need has given structure to our ambitions. We needed to build shelter, acquire food, treat disease, and develop new technologies. Even when these tasks were demanding, succeeding at them gave us a sense of competence and agency.

If a superintelligence can accomplish nearly all of these things more effectively than we can, humanity may have to revisit what it means to contribute. I am concerned that some people would experience this as a loss of identity.

An artist may wonder why they should paint when an AI can create technically superior images in seconds. A scientist may question the value of years of research when a machine can reach the same discovery almost immediately. A student may struggle to find drive when the skills they are developing are already surpassed by artificial systems.

These concerns do not imply that human effort would become objectively worthless. They suggest that our current methods of measuring achievement may become less satisfying. However, I also think there is reason for hope; perhaps future generations will develop a different understanding of purpose, one less dependent on producing results that others cannot achieve. While not proven, it may certainly be the case that this aim-achievement relationship is not inherent to being human, but rather a mindset we have developed in “recent” human history.

People already engage in activities that are not strictly required. We participate in sports even though machines can move objects more accurately. We climb mountains even when a helicopter can reach the summit. We create music even though recordings can reproduce performances perfectly. The value of these activities often comes from participating in them, not merely from securing the most efficient outcome.

Perhaps the same principle could help shape human life in a world of superintelligence. An artist might continue painting because the creative process matters to them. A scientist might keep investigating questions out of curiosity, even when an AI already knows the answers. People might place greater emphasis on relationships, exploration, creativity, and experiences that cannot be measured solely by productivity.

They may find that the value of a song rests not in its acoustic perfection, but in the fact that another human sang it.

I find this possibility hopeful, though I suspect the transition would be difficult. Our current society places immense importance on competence, productivity, and achievement. Many of us have spent our lives learning to measure our worth through what we can achieve. Losing that foundation, even in exchange for a more comfortable existence, could be psychologically disturbing.

This is why I believe the arrival of aligned superintelligence would not mark the end of humanity's philosophical problems. It could instead introduce a completely new set of questions about meaning, identity, and the role of effort in a fulfilling life.

The sparrows might eventually learn to enjoy perching in the evening sun. But they would first have to learn what it means to live without constructing their own nests.

## VII. The Unfinished Fable
Bostrom's fable leaves us with a question that I believe is becoming increasingly relevant: should the sparrows have searched for an owl before understanding how they could safely coexist with it?

To me, the lesson is not that humanity should give up on the pursuit of artificial intelligence. I believe the potential benefits of superintelligence are too important to ignore, and I consider its eventual development highly likely.

However, I also believe that creating intelligence beyond our own brings responsibilities that cannot be addressed simply by making our systems more capable.

The Orthogonality Thesis and Instrumental Convergence Thesis show why intelligence alone does not guarantee cooperation with human values. The potential for conscious AI raises moral questions regarding the rights and experiences of the entities we might create. Constraining AI presents a challenge, while instilling motivations in systems we do not fully understand remains barely fathomable. And even if we successfully align superintelligence with human interests, we may still have to reconsider how we find meaning in a world where our intellectual labor is no longer indispensable.

**We tend to consider artificial intelligence in terms of what it can do for us, without giving equal attention to how its existence might change our relationship with the world.**

I believe that solving the control problem requires more than stopping an AI from acting dangerously. It requires developing systems that can act in accordance with human values while permitting those values to evolve. This is why I am especially interested in Coherent Extrapolated Volition, despite its unresolved challenges.

At the same time, I think we need to recognize that safety alone may not be adequate. A future in which humanity is perfectly protected yet has lost its sense of agency would still raise important questions about what kind of world we have created.

Ultimately, I do not think our objective should be to prevent artificial intelligence from becoming more capable than humans. Rather, it should be to ensure that greater intelligence helps create a future in which humanity can continue to develop, make choices, and find purpose.

The sparrows were not mistaken in imagining a better life with the help of an owl. Their error was assuming that the benefits would take care of themselves. If we are going to create something more intelligent than ourselves, we should devote as much thought to how we will live alongside it as we devote to how we will build it.

Undoubtedly, the most important question is not whether we can find an owl, but whether we are ready for what happens when we do.
